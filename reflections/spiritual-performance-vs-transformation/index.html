Spiritual Performance vs. Spiritual Transformation

When the image of being spiritual becomes more important than actually being transformed

When Spirituality Becomes an Identity

There is a danger on the spiritual path that is rarely talked about: the danger of becoming attached to the image of being spiritual.

The ego is clever. It can take almost anything and turn it into another reason to feel important, special, separate, or superior. And spirituality is no exception. In fact, the spiritual path can sometimes give the ego an entirely new costume to wear.

Opening the Door Is Not Walking Through It

We may begin with a sincere desire to know God, discover our true nature, heal ourselves, understand consciousness, or find the deeper meaning of life. We meditate, pray, study sacred teachings, attend workshops, visit temples and ashrams, chant mantras, perform pujas, burn incense, light candles, collect crystals, participate in sound baths, smudge our homes, practice energy work and explore one spiritual experience after another. None of these things are necessarily wrong. They can all have value. They can open a door.

But opening the door is not the same thing as walking through it.

What Is Actually Changing Inside Me?

At some point, the spiritual seeker has to ask a much harder question: What is actually changing inside me?

Am I becoming more loving? More honest? More forgiving? Humbler? More patient? More compassionate? More willing to admit when I am wrong? Am I becoming less attached to being right? Less interested in recognition? Less concerned with proving how spiritually advanced I am?

Or have I simply become very good at looking spiritual?

When the Ego Learns Spiritual Language

This is where the ego becomes particularly dangerous. The ordinary ego wants money, recognition, power, status, attention, beauty, success and approval. The spiritual ego can want exactly the same things, only dressed in robes, crystals, sacred language and enlightened terminology.

We can begin to believe that because we have had a profound meditation, a mystical experience, a near-death experience, a vision, a kundalini awakening, a psychic experience, an unusual dream, an encounter with what we believe to be a higher being, or some other extraordinary event, we have somehow arrived.

We have reached the mountaintop.

And once we believe we have arrived, we often stop climbing.

The ego whispers, “You are different now. You understand things other people don’t. You have been chosen. You have special knowledge. You have special powers. People need you. You are here to save them.”

Suddenly we are not simply a seeker anymore. We are a teacher, healer, channeler, prophet, guru, savior, master, spiritual leader or authority.

There is nothing wrong with teaching, healing, guiding or serving others. These can be beautiful expressions of spiritual life. The danger comes when the identity becomes more important than the transformation.

We can become so busy trying to change the world that we forget to change ourselves.

We want everyone else to awaken, but we do not want to look honestly at our own anger. We want to teach unconditional love while keeping a list of people we cannot forgive. We speak about Oneness while dividing people into those who are awakened and those who are not. We teach humility while secretly needing admiration. We talk about surrender while insisting that our interpretation of God, consciousness, energy or truth is the correct one.

This is the great spiritual trap.

The ego does not necessarily disappear because we become spiritual. Sometimes it simply becomes more sophisticated.

It learns the vocabulary of enlightenment.

It learns how to talk about vibration, consciousness, manifestation, ascension, energy, karma, chakras, frequencies, soul contracts and higher dimensions. It can quote scripture and sacred texts. It can know the Upanishads, the Bhagavad Gita, the Bible, Buddhist teachings or the words of great saints and sages.

Knowing Truth Is Not Living Truth

But knowing about truth is not the same as living truth.

We can memorize thousands of words about love and still be unloving.

We can read everything ever written about forgiveness and still carry resentment.

We can speak endlessly about Oneness and still believe that we are spiritually superior to someone else.

We can meditate for an hour every morning and spend the rest of the day complaining, judging, manipulating, gossiping, lying or finding fault.

We can have spiritual experiences and still have an ego that needs to be seen.

The Self Beneath the Costume

This is why the Eastern traditions have always placed such importance on the purification of the heart and mind. Spiritual realization is not simply the accumulation of experiences. It is a movement inward, toward the quiet place where the false identities begin to fall away.

The great teachings of India point us again and again toward the discovery of the Self beneath the personality. The Atman is not the costume we have created. It is not our title, our reputation, our spiritual history, our number of followers, our psychic abilities or the unusual things that have happened to us.

The deeper Self does not need to announce itself.

It does not need to prove that it is enlightened.

It does not need to be special.

There is a beautiful paradox here. The closer we come to the Divine, the less important the personal ego becomes.

The spiritual journey is not necessarily about becoming somebody extraordinary. It may actually be about becoming less interested in being somebody at all.

This is why silence is so powerful.

When we become quiet, we begin to notice how much of our inner life is simply noise. The mind is constantly creating stories about who we are, what we know, what we have experienced, what others think of us and what we should become. We collect identities almost as quickly as we collect spiritual objects.

The Deeper Work Is Often Emptying

But the deeper spiritual work is often the emptying.

Emptying the heart of hatred.

Emptying the mind of unnecessary judgment.

Emptying ourselves of the need to be right.

Emptying ourselves of resentment.

Emptying ourselves of the desire to impress.

Emptying ourselves of the constant need for recognition.

Emptying ourselves of the belief that we already have all the answers.

This does not mean becoming empty in the sense of becoming nothing. It means making room for something deeper.

The Inner Light has always been there.

We do not manufacture it. We uncover it.

We do not become Divine. We discover the Divine that was never separate from us.

This is where the Eastern understanding of enlightenment becomes so different from the ego’s version of enlightenment. The ego wants an achievement. The ego wants a certificate from the universe saying, “You have arrived.”

But realization is not a trophy.

It is a continual awakening.

Even a person who has had profound spiritual experiences still has a human life to live. There are relationships to navigate, responsibilities to honor, mistakes to acknowledge, wounds to heal, promises to keep and difficult truths to face.

Spirituality Has to Come Home

This is where morality and ethics become part of spirituality.

If our meditation does not eventually change the way we treat people, something is missing.

If our prayers do not make us kinder, something is missing.

If our spiritual studies do not make us more honest, something is missing.

If our experiences do not make us more compassionate, something is missing.

If our knowledge makes us feel superior to others, we have misunderstood the purpose of knowledge.

The spiritual life cannot simply be what happens on the meditation cushion, in the temple, at the retreat, during ceremony or inside a sacred space. It has to come home with us.

It has to enter the kitchen.

It has to enter the marriage.

It has to enter the friendship.

It has to enter the workplace.

It has to enter the way we speak to strangers.

It has to enter the way we treat someone who disagrees with us.

It has to enter the way we respond when life does not give us what we want.

That is where the real practice begins.

Life Itself Becomes the Ashram

It is relatively easy to feel peaceful while sitting quietly in a beautiful meditation room with incense burning and sacred music playing. The real test comes when someone pushes our buttons, insults us, betrays us, disagrees with us or refuses to give us what we want.

Then we discover where we really are.

Life itself becomes the ashram.

Our relationships become the meditation.

Our difficulties become the teachers.

Our mistakes become opportunities for self-correction.

Our suffering can become a doorway into compassion.

The Quiet Work

This is why the spiritual seeker must be willing to look at the parts of themselves they would rather not see. The shadow does not disappear because we call ourselves lightworkers. Anger does not disappear because we talk about positive vibration. Fear does not disappear because we burn sage. The ego does not disappear because we wear spiritual clothing.

We have to meet ourselves honestly.

This is the quiet work.

And it is often not glamorous.

There may be no audience.

There may be no photograph.

There may be no social media post.

Nobody may ever know that we chose forgiveness instead of revenge, honesty instead of convenience, humility instead of recognition, silence instead of an argument, compassion instead of judgment.

Yet those moments may be far more spiritually significant than anything we perform publicly.

Spiritual Powers Are Not Wisdom

The Eastern traditions have also warned about something else: spiritual powers, or siddhis.

Extraordinary abilities may arise through deep spiritual practice. Different traditions describe psychic perception, unusual states of consciousness, healing abilities, visions and other phenomena. Whether one understands these experiences literally, psychologically or metaphysically, the important spiritual warning remains the same: powers are not the same thing as wisdom.

A person can have extraordinary experiences and still be governed by an extraordinary ego.

A person can know things and still not know themselves.

A person can influence others and still have very little inner peace.

A person can be called a healer and still need healing.

A person can be called a guru and still have lessons to learn.

A person can speak about God and still not know how to love the person standing directly in front of them.

The siddhi can become another possession of the ego.

“I have something you don’t have.”

“I know something you don’t know.”

“I can see what you cannot see.”

“I have been chosen.”

That is separation, not Oneness.

We Are Still Becoming

True spiritual wisdom seems to move in the opposite direction. It becomes quieter. Softer. Less interested in proving itself.

The truly wise person does not need to convince everyone of their wisdom.

There is a humility that comes from realizing how much we still do not know.

The older I become, the more I understand that the spiritual journey is not about collecting answers. It is about becoming spacious enough to receive deeper questions.

Every time we think we have the entire truth, life has a way of showing us another door.

This is why I believe we must be careful about declaring ourselves finished.

We are not finished.

None of us should be too comfortable with our spiritual identity. The moment we begin saying, “I am enlightened,” “I have all the answers,” or “I know exactly how this works,” we should probably become very quiet and look inward.

The Divine is much larger than our personal understanding of the Divine.

The Truth is much larger than our interpretation of Truth.

Consciousness is much larger than our vocabulary about consciousness.

And the human being standing before us may be carrying a depth of Divine mystery that we cannot see.

Perhaps this is what humility really means.

Humility is not thinking less of ourselves. It is becoming less obsessed with ourselves.

It is remembering that whatever light we have is not ours to possess.

We are vessels, not owners.

We are students as well as teachers.

We are seekers as well as guides.

We are still becoming.

The Goal Is to Become Real

The real spiritual path may therefore be much simpler than the ego wants it to be. Love more. Forgive more. Tell the truth. Keep your word. Control your appetites. Stop complaining. Stop finding fault. Serve others. Become kinder. Examine yourself. Make amends when you are wrong. Sit quietly. Listen. Let go. Be grateful. And keep looking inward.

This is where spirituality becomes real.

Not when we look spiritual.

When we become spiritual.

Not when other people recognize our light.

When we stop needing them to.

Not when we accumulate more spiritual experiences.

When we allow those experiences to transform the way we live.

The goal is not to become spiritually special. The goal is to become real.

And perhaps, beneath all the rituals, teachings, ceremonies, crystals, mantras, meditations, prayers, visions, energies and experiences, the great invitation has always been the same:

Be still.

Empty yourself.

Look within.

Find the Light that was never separate from you.

Then let that Light change the way you live.

That is the work.

And the work never really ends.

A Question to Carry With You

What has your spiritual practice actually changed in the way you love, speak, forgive, listen, respond, and live?
